# Rover Games, Mission Scenarios, and Learning Design

This file covers three things for the rover concept, where the player receives a mission, designs a rover, has it delivered by a provided lander, and must keep it alive and productive on the surface. Part A surveys existing rover games, simulators, classroom activities, and competitions. Part B is a library of mission cards grounded in real science goals and real sites. Part C turns NASA educator practice and NGSS into design guidance, and sketches a sol-by-sol survival loop.

Researched November 14, 2026. General mission-design games (KSP, Mars Horizon, Marsbound, Build It Yourself: Satellite!, and others) are already compared in [landscape research](../landscape-precedents-and-judging.md#comparable-games-and-tools); this file only extends that work for rovers. Rover history, subsystem values, and environment numbers belong to [01](01-rover-missions-history.md), [02](02-rover-subsystems.md), and [03](03-surface-environments-and-operations.md). Items marked *unverified* rest on a single or weak source.

## A. Comparable products

Feature key used in the tables: **Des** rover design choices · **Pwr** power or energy budget · **Env** environment hazards (dust, cold, night, slopes) · **Wear** damage, wear, or repair · **Cmd** command planning or time delay · **Sci** science targets or scoring · **Surv** survival over many days.

### Gap analysis

**Our analysis.** Across 30-plus products, rover games split into four groups:

| Group | Examples | What they do well | What is missing |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Drive-and-sample games | Explore Mars, Experience Curiosity, JPL Mars Rover, Code a Mars Rover Driving Game, Red Rover | Command planning, science targets, time delay | No design phase, no power or survival over time |
| Build sandboxes | KSP, Space Engineers, Take On Mars | Free-form building with power parts | Piloting effort, unrealistic or absent environment, no learning scaffolding |
| Survival and maintenance games | Surviving Mars, Out of ContRover, jam games, Rover Mechanic Simulator | Dust, storms, breakdowns, energy anxiety | The player did not design the machine, so failure teaches nothing about design choices |
| Classroom activities and competitions | Board game, JPL robotics lessons, HERC, Lunabotics, URC | Explicit criteria and constraints, roles, efficiency scoring, review gates | Physical, slow, mostly middle-school level; no multi-sol consequences |

No product found connects **mission objective → rover design → delivery → multi-sol survival where the consequences trace back to specific design choices → debrief comparing with a real rover**. The board game's daily command budget, Lunabotics' per-kilogram and per-energy scoring, Surviving Mars' storm and maintenance events, and Red Rover's delayed command packets are each proven in isolation. Combining them in a short browser session for 14 to 18 year olds is the open position. A second gap: **no comparable product lets the player choose the destination's challenge (night, cold, dust, low gravity, relay communications) and see that the right rover differs by world**, including the case where a rover is the wrong answer (Titan's Dragonfly is a rotorcraft).

### Card format

Each card has a destination, a real science objective with a source, the key environmental challenge, suggested requirements, a difficulty tier, and the real mission it mirrors. **Requirement numbers are game-balance placeholders, not engineering values;** they use game units (map tiles, samples, sols, lunar nights) so that file 03 can supply the physical numbers. Tiers: **1** tutorial, **2** standard, **3** hard, **4** expert or stretch.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Climb the Layers** | Mars, Gale Crater, lower Mount Sharp | Assess whether Mars "ever had an environment capable of supporting microbial life"; Mount Sharp's clay-bearing unit records water history ([NASA](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/msl-curiosity/science/)) | Gentle slopes, rough rocks (wheel wear), a long-lived mission | Drive 6 tiles uphill; analyze 3 layers; survive 30 sols | 1 | Curiosity |

| 11 | **Tiny Moon, Tiny Gravity** | Phobos (Mars moon) | Analyze surface composition and texture and help choose the sample site for the mothership; about three months of operation ([DLR](https://www.dlr.de/en/research-and-transfer/projects-and-missions/mmx)) | Very low gravity (wheels slip and the rover can bounce); short day and night cycle | Drive 2 tiles without losing contact with the ground; scout 3 candidate sites | 3 | Idefix on JAXA's MMX. DLR reports launch on October 19, 2026, but its page is internally inconsistent *(launch unverified)*; landing expected 2028–2029 |
| 12 | **Wrong Vehicle?** | Titan, Shangri-La dunes toward Selk crater | Investigate prebiotic chemistry and habitability; Selk is an impact site where liquid water may once have existed briefly ([Universe Today](https://www.universetoday.com/articles/dragonfly-is-going-to-titan-on-a-falcon-heavy)) | Dunes, cold, very slow communication; flight may beat driving | Reach 3 sites; the debrief reveals Dragonfly flies | 4 (bonus) | Dragonfly rotorcraft, launch planned July 2028 |

**Our analysis on sequencing.** A good first campaign is 1 → 2 → 8 → 7, which teaches Mars basics, then the lunar night, then darkness and relays. Cards 5 and 12 work as "twist" cards that teach how science and engineering conclusions change. Card 10 needs mechanics (descent, tethers) that a first version should skip.

## C. Educational design guidance

### C1. Common misconceptions to design against

Sourced research on student ideas about rovers specifically is thin; most rows pair a documented general misconception with a real rover fact. Rows marked "our analysis" are hypotheses to test with playtesters.

| Misconception | What is true | How the game can confront it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "There is no gravity in space, so none on the Moon." | Listed as common for grades 5–8 ([CfA](https://www.cfa.harvard.edu/news/what-do-students-know)); about 11% of introductory college astronomy students in one study predicted a pen on the Moon would float forever ([UCT study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1409.2363)) | Show weight on each world in the design screen; on Phobos show the rover hopping rather than floating |
| "A Martian dust storm could knock a rover over" (as in *The Martian*). | The atmosphere is about 1% as dense as Earth's, so a 60 mph gust feels like about 6 mph; the real hazards are dimmed sunlight and grit in moving parts ([NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/solar-system/the-fact-and-fiction-of-martian-dust-storms/)) | Storms cut solar input and add wear, never knock the rover over |
| "Mars has no air." | NASA calls the atmosphere thin, mostly carbon dioxide, nitrogen, and argon ([NASA Mars facts](https://science.nasa.gov/mars/facts/)) | Atmosphere affects dust, heat loss, and parachutes (lander), shown in tooltips |
| "Rovers are driven live with a joystick." | Signals take roughly 3 to 22 minutes each way ([JPL board game](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/edu/resources/lesson-plan/mars-rover-driver-board-game/)) | Plan a sol's commands, then watch them execute; no real-time steering |
| "Rovers are slow because their motors are weak." | Autonomous hazard checks forced stops to take and analyze stereo images; earlier rovers drove 4 to 10 times slower autonomously than on human-planned drives. Perseverance's speed is now limited by its motors' rotation rate, set for climbing rather than speed ([JPL Robotics](https://robotics.jpl.nasa.gov/media/documents/2023-rapid-traverse.pdf)) | A "drive mode" choice: blind drive (fast, risky) versus autonomous hazard checks (slow, safe) |
| "Solar power works the same everywhere." | Opportunity's last contact came during a 2018 planet-wide dust storm that stopped it recharging ([NASA JPL](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-opportunity-rover-mission-on-mars-comes-to-end/)) | Power input changes with world, season, dust on panels, and storms (numbers from file 03) |
| "A rover can just switch off for the night and wake up later." | India's Pragyan rover was put to sleep before the lunar night and never responded again ([Live Science](https://livescience.com/space/space-exploration/indias-chandrayaan-3-moon-lander-fails-to-answer-wake-up-call-may-be-dead-for-good)); Yutu-2 carries a radioisotope heater for the night | Night needs a heat plan, not only a battery plan |
| "The dark streaks on Mars are flowing water." | A 2017 study points to dry granular flows ([JPL](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/recurring-martian-streaks-flowing-sand-not-water/)) | Card 5 reveals the revised interpretation in the debrief |
| "A rover is always the right vehicle." *(our analysis)* | Titan's Dragonfly is a rotorcraft | Card 12; a "vehicle type" choice in later versions |
| "A mission ends when its design life is up." *(our analysis)* | Opportunity was designed for about 90 sols and operated from 2004 to 2019 ([NASA JPL](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-opportunity-rover-mission-on-mars-comes-to-end/)) | Bonus score for every sol beyond the requirement; "extended mission" ending |

### C3. How NASA frames rover design challenges

| Practice | Evidence | Apply in the game |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A named engineering design cycle | NASA's BEST guides use Ask, Imagine, Plan, Create, Test, Improve; a later version adds Share and renames Experiment to Test ([NASA BEST 6–8](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/630754main_NASAsBESTActivityGuide6-8.pdf), [Texas State EPDC](https://www.txstate-epdc.net/models-of-the-engineering-design-process/)) | Label the game phases with these words: Ask (briefing), Imagine/Plan (design), Create (assemble), Test (sim and landing), Improve (redesign), Share (debrief card) |
| Criteria and constraints listed separately | JPL robotics lessons list "criteria for success" and "engineering constraints" as two lists | Briefing shows two columns: "Must achieve" and "Must stay within" |
| Roles that argue | Board game: Scientist wants more rocks, Engineer keeps the plan within constraints | Single player: two advisor voices give conflicting advice; 2-player: split the roles |
| Communication as design | Rover Races treats the command protocol as the design problem | Command queue limits per sol; delayed execution |
| Review gates | HERC requires four readiness reviews before the excursion | A pre-delivery "Design Review" checklist that flags failures before launch |
| Iteration expected | The self-driving rover lesson asks students to accept failure and iterate | Free redesign after a failed mission, with the cause highlighted |
| Efficiency scoring | Lunabotics normalizes productivity by mass and energy *(team source)* | Score science per kilogram and per watt-hour, not only total science |

### C4. Formative feedback

Hattie and Timperley's model frames feedback as three questions: where am I going (feed up), how am I going (feed back), and where to next (feed forward), with feed forward among the most powerful ([summary of Hattie & Timperley 2007](https://sola.kau.se/keeponteaching/about-feedback-feed-up-feed-back-och-feed-forward/?lang=en)).

| Question | Game moment | Example (our design) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Feed up | Briefing and a persistent objective tracker | "Collect 5 samples from 3 rock types. You have 2." |
| Feed back | End of each sol | "Battery ended at 18%. Heaters used most of tonight's energy." |
| Feed forward | Debrief and redesign screen | "One more panel would have kept you driving through the storm. It costs 4 kg, which you have spare." |
| Prediction before outcome | Before sending a sol plan | Ask "Will the battery last the night?" (yes/no), then show the result; predictions that are wrong are strong learning moments *(our analysis)* |
| Explain the failure, never just "Game over" | Rover loss | A cause chain: dust on panels → low charge → heaters off → electronics too cold |

### C5. Showing trade-offs visually

**Our analysis**, informed by the "live budgets with hard limits" lesson in [landscape research](../landscape-precedents-and-judging.md#design-lessons).

| Technique | What it shows | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stacked budget bars (mass, power, cost) with a margin zone | Each part's share and how close the design is to a limit | Colour the margin, not the parts, so the eye goes to risk |
| 24-hour energy strip per sol | Battery level across day and night, with solar input and loads | The single most important graph for HS-PS3-1 |
| "Ghost" comparison | The previous design's result drawn faintly behind the current one | Makes iteration visible |
| Capability tags on parts | "Climbs steeper," "Survives night," "Reads organics" | Lets beginners reason in outcomes before numbers |
| Two-axis trade chart | Science achieved versus risk, one dot per design attempt | Introduces the idea that no design is best on both |
| Real-rover overlay in the debrief | "Your rover is closest to Yutu-2" | Echoes the Build It Yourself payoff noted in [landscape research](../landscape-precedents-and-judging.md#design-lessons) |

Avoid radar (spider) charts for beginners; area distortion makes them hard to read *(our analysis)*.

### Sol-based survival loop and event deck

**Our analysis.** Environment numbers (sunlight, temperatures, night length, delay) come from file 03; subsystem values come from file 02.

**Core loop per sol (or per lunar-day block):**

1. **Morning report.** Battery, temperature state, part health, dust on panels, data waiting to send.
2. **Plan.** Fill a limited command queue (drive, image, sample, drill, send data, heat, sleep, clean panels if a part allows it). Commands cost energy and time. Communication windows limit how many commands can be uploaded and how much data goes home.
3. **Execute.** Commands run with no player control; hazards resolve.
4. **Night.** Heaters draw from the battery or a radioisotope source; if energy runs out, parts take cold damage.
5. **Event draw.** Zero or one event, weighted by world and season.
6. **Score update.** Science points banked only when the data reaches Earth.

**Event deck (examples).**

| Event | Worlds | Player responses | Lesson | Real anchor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Regional dust storm | Mars | Sleep, cut loads, wait it out | Solar dependence | Opportunity, 2018 |
| Dust devil passes | Mars | None needed; panels cleaned (bonus) | Not all events are bad | Recorded rover panel cleanings *(details in file 03)* |
| Wheel damage on sharp rocks | Mars, Moon | Change route; slow drive mode | Terrain wear; route choice | Curiosity's wheels (file 01) |
| Embedded in soft sand | Mars | Spend sols backing out; or abandon a target | Risk of shortcuts | Spirit (file 01) |
| Relay satellite unavailable | Moon far side, Mars | Store data; delay upload | Communication architecture | Chang'e 4 relay |
| Solar conjunction | Mars | No commands for several sols; preload a safe plan | Planning ahead | Red Rover's Sun-blocking mechanic |
| Lunar night falls | Moon | Park facing a good direction; enter sleep | Night survival plan | Yutu-2 and Pragyan |
| Computer reset | Any | Lose a sol; safe mode | Fault protection | (file 01) |
| Surprise discovery | Any | Detour to a high-value target at energy cost | Science versus safety | Card 2 sample choices |
| Instrument degradation | Any | Prioritize remaining measurements | Graceful degradation | (file 02) |

**Our analysis on pacing:** a session should cover 15 to 30 turns, which suggests compressing long missions into turns of several sols, with storms and nights as the main punctuation.

## Game implications

**Our recommendations**, drawn from the gap analysis and the guidance above.

1. **Make the design-to-consequence link the core.** Every failure event should name the design choice that caused or prevented it. This is the gap no comparable product fills.
2. **Use a daily command budget.** The JPL board game's command limit per day is a proven, low-complexity way to make energy and time scarce. Tie the budget to battery state.
3. **Bank science only when downlinked.** This extends the [market-gap finding](../landscape-precedents-and-judging.md#market-gap) that data budgets are rare in comparable games, and makes communication a rover trade too.
4. **Model night and storms as the main threats**, not combat or random damage. These are the real threats (Opportunity, Pragyan), and they force the most important design choices: power source, battery size, heaters.
5. **Offer a "drive mode" trade** (fast blind drive versus slow autonomous drive) to answer "why so slow" through play.
6. **Score efficiency.** Report science per kilogram and per unit of energy, as Lunabotics does, plus sols survived beyond the requirement.
7. **Structure the design phase with NASA's words.** Ask, Imagine, Plan, Create, Test, Improve, Share; separate "Must achieve" and "Must stay within" lists; a review gate before delivery.
8. **Debrief with feed forward and a real-rover match.** "Closest to Yutu-2" plus one concrete improvement.
9. **Plan for two players now, build later.** Split Scientist and Engineer roles over one shared budget, as in the board game.
10. **Cite the source on each mission card** inside the game, matching the pattern seen in past Space Apps winners ([landscape research](../landscape-precedents-and-judging.md#patterns-across-these-precedents-analysis)).

**Suggested first-version scope for a solo developer:**

| In scope | Out of scope for version 1 |
| --- | --- |
| Three mission cards: 1 (Climb the Layers, tutorial), 2 (Delta Detectives), 8 (Far Side Pioneer) | Cards 6, 9, 10, 12 (polar night, long traverse, caves, flight) |
| Design screen with about 5 subsystems and 2 or 3 options each, live mass, power, and cost bars | Free-form 3D part placement |
| 2D top-down tile map; turn-based sols; command queue of 4 to 6 slots | Physics driving or real-time control |
| One energy model (solar or radioisotope, battery, heaters) with a day and night strip graph | Detailed thermal or terrain mechanics |
| Event deck of about 8 events | Procedural terrain generation |
| Debrief: cause chain, one feed-forward tip, closest real rover | Accounts, leaderboards, multiplayer |
| Browser build, keyboard and mouse, playable in 15 to 20 minutes | Mobile-first layout, VR |
