## Official brief: purpose and scope

**Create an interactive game for designing, managing, and simulating a complete space mission, with engineering decisions, limited resources, and visible consequences.** The educational gap is understanding how mission subsystems interact, beyond isolated lessons about rockets or orbits. Students and aspiring engineers should be able to experiment with integrated mission decisions and learn from successful and unsuccessful outcomes.

The challenge is labeled **Intermediate** and **Beginner/Youth**, with **Games** as its subject.

The brief explicitly labels its Potential Considerations as optional. Its examples guide scope selection; they are not a mandatory feature checklist.

Source: [official Details tab](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/2026/challenges/space-mission-design-game/).

## Core challenge outcomes

These outcomes unpack the central assignment. The evidence column is our proposed way to demonstrate fulfillment, not a NASA-prescribed test.

| ID | Outcome from the assignment | Evidence to prepare |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CORE-01 | Deliver an interactive game. | The player can act and observe responses, rather than only read information or watch an animation. |
| CORE-02 | Let participants design a mission. | Player choices determine the mission configuration. |
| CORE-03 | Let participants manage a mission. | Decisions affect the use of constrained mission resources. |
| CORE-04 | Simulate a complete mission. | Show how the chosen configuration leads to a mission outcome across the scenario’s defined lifecycle. |
| CORE-05 | Include consequential engineering decisions. | Explain at least one causal connection between a design choice and its consequences elsewhere in the mission. |
| CORE-06 | Make success depend on choices under limited resources. | Relate the final outcome to the player’s decisions and mission objectives. |

“Complete” is not accompanied by a prescribed number of phases, destinations, or numerical fidelity threshold in the brief. Our interpretation should define a coherent beginning-to-end scenario and disclose its boundaries.

### Scope choices

The brief does not prescribe a programming language, game engine, hosting provider, 2D/3D format, destination, crewed/uncrewed mission, real-time data feed, or named physics engine. It does not require integrating every listed resource, building a production mission-analysis tool, or implementing every optional subsystem. These are implementation decisions to document once we select a concept.

The resource list supplements the brief. An example tool’s capabilities, screen size, or historical challenge requirements should not be treated as additional 2026 requirements.

## Resources

All nine references below appear in the official Resources content. Descriptions summarize that listing, supplemented by direct review where noted below. Suggested uses are our planning guidance, not commitments to integrate a tool.

### Mission engineering references

**[Design and Develop Science Missions](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/250323-design-and-develop-science-mission.pdf?emrc=a454cf)**

Systems engineering from scientific need through requirements, design, verification, and implementation. Distinguishes launch, space, ground, and user segments.

Suggested use: Check whether our mission representation connects spacecraft decisions to ground operations and the people using the results.

Reviewed directly (local copy: [nasa-design-and-develop-science-missions-2025.pdf](reference-pdfs/nasa-design-and-develop-science-missions-2025.pdf)). It is a 25-slide NASA Small Spacecraft Systems Virtual Institute webinar by Julie Levri, flight systems engineer, from the *Access to Space for All* systems-engineering series, dated March 26, 2025. Its process spine is **Identify Need & Goals → Define Concept → Define Requirements → Design → Build & Verify → Implement**. It defines science missions as Earth science, heliophysics, planetary science, and astrophysics, and it stresses "begin with the end in mind": start from the data the user segment needs, then derive requirements. Its worked example is BioNutrients-2 on the ISS:

- **Need:** produce nutrients in place, with minimal mass, power, and volume.
- **Three goals:** grow yeast, produce beta carotene, and reduce mass and volume compared with BioNutrients-1.
- **Measurable requirements:** at least 12 replicate samples, at least 30 mL per sample, at least 3 µg beta carotene per mL, a sample container under 250 g, and less than 300 mL of occupied volume.
- **Segments:** a launch, space, ground, and user segment for the mission.

The webinar adds a rule: whenever you write a requirement, also plan how it will be verified. For example, check the 250 g limit by weighing an empty container. It cites the NASA Systems Engineering Handbook (SP-2016-6105 Rev 2), including the Concept of Operations outline in Appendix S, and NPR 7123.1D. Example missions include PUNCH, a constellation of four small satellites, and Dawn.

Game implication: the six steps can form the game's phases, and each requirement can carry a pass/fail check that the post-mission report evaluates.

**[General Mission Analysis Tool (GMAT)](https://software.nasa.gov/software/GSC-19468-1)**

Open-source mission design, optimization, and navigation software spanning Earth orbit, lunar/libration-point, and deep-space missions. The challenge listing describes trajectory propagation, maneuver/launch-window analysis, and analytical outputs. NASA’s catalog lists Windows, Linux, and macOS.

Suggested use: Evaluate whether selected calculations need an external reference or precomputed validation. Tool adoption is a separate scope decision.

**[Concept Maturity Levels (CMLs)](https://dataverse.jpl.nasa.gov/dataset.xhtml?persistentId=hdl:2014/44299)**

JPL framework for progressively developing mission concepts. The listing emphasizes CML 1–4: early ideas, feasibility, trade studies, and increasingly concrete designs.

Suggested use: Ask what evidence is needed before a player or the team treats a mission concept as feasible.

Reviewed directly (local copy: [jpl-concept-maturity-levels-2013.pdf](reference-pdfs/jpl-concept-maturity-levels-2013.pdf)). The paper is by Wessen, Borden, Ziemer, and Kwok (JPL, © 2013 Caltech). Its levels:

| CML | Name | Meaning (condensed) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Cocktail Napkin | The science questions and the observations needed are stated, with a rough sketch of the concept. Only four items count here: science objective, unique characteristics, approximate launch timeframe, and target cost class. |
| 2 | Initial Feasibility | Lower-level objectives and key performance parameters are set, and first-order calculations show whether the concept is viable. |
| 3 | Trade Space | Trades among the spacecraft, the ground system, and the mission design show how science return, cost, and risk relate. |
| 4 | Point Design | One design and cost is chosen and broken down to major subsystems with acceptable margins and reserves. This is the level of JPL's Team X. |
| 5–9 | Baseline Concept → Integrated Concept → Preliminary Implementation Baseline → PDR → CDR | Formal project maturity. These levels are outside a game's scope. |

Ideas that map directly onto game mechanics:

- **Iterate between CML 2 and CML 4.** Teams often return from a point design to the trade space when problems appear. The paper calls this loop a strength, which supports a redesign-and-resimulate loop (EDU-07).
- **Science partials.** Track how science return changes with a mission parameter, such as spacecraft mass, data volume, or orbital period. A game could display this sensitivity directly.
- **Skipping trade-space exploration has a cost.** It produced designs with lower science return and inefficient spacecraft and ground systems. A game could reward players who explore alternatives before committing (MIS-06).
- **The CML matrix spans 24 dimensions in five groups:** Science (2: objectives and data products), Technical (13), Management (5), Cost (1), and Other (3). NASA reviews science first, then TMCO (Technical, Management, Cost, Other). The matrix treats the spacecraft and the ground system as one integrated system.
- **Checklists use status colors.** Each item is green (complete), yellow (in progress), or red (not started), and a concept is at a level when about 80% of its items are green. Engineering judgment still applies: missing a Science Traceability Matrix, Level 1 requirements, or a Master Equipment List outweighs the percentage. This could serve as a template for a readiness gate before launch.
- **Self-assessment tends to be optimistic.** The paper recommends independent review. In co-op play, a teammate or an automatic check could act as the reviewer.
- **Teams lack the time, funds, and people to fix every deficiency,** so they prioritize the critical ones. This mirrors a game's limited budget.

**[Mission Design Lab (MDL)](https://etd.gsfc.nasa.gov/capabilities/integrated-design-center/mission-design-lab/)**

Collaborative concept engineering, from brainstorming and feasibility/risk assessment through trade studies and point designs. Disciplines produce requirements, alternatives, mass/power/data estimates, models, costs, and risks, covering ground systems, launch, flight design, operations, and mission end.

Suggested use: Structure the dependencies and decision records behind the simulation.

### Visualization and learning references

**[NASA Mission Viz](https://nasa.github.io/mission-viz/)**

Interactive 3D representation of celestial bodies, spacecraft, trajectories, and mission timing. The listing describes viewpoint changes and spatial/temporal exploration.

Suggested use: Examine how visual context can explain a numerical mission result.

**[A Learning Game About Telescopes: Build It Yourself](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/game-build-it-yourself/)**

Science objectives drive wavelength, instrument, and optics choices. Incompatible choices can be unavailable; players revise before launch and progress through three levels. Results connect designs to real missions. The page specifies a 1024 × 768 minimum screen and limited built-in accessibility.

Suggested use: Compare our intended learning contribution and assess device/accessibility needs independently.

**[CubeSat 101](https://www3.nasa.gov/sites/default/files/atoms/files/nasa_csli_cubesat_101_508.pdf)**

Introductory guide for first-time CubeSat developers, covering mission phases from the concept of operations through launch and operation.

Suggested use: Identify development and operating considerations if we later select a small-satellite scenario.

**[Visualize a Space Mission Using Virtual Reality — 2023 challenge](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/2023/challenges/visualize-a-space-mission-using-virtual-reality/)**

Historical challenge linking mission concept, trajectories, timeline, and 3D visualization in an interactive VR experience.

Suggested use: Study an earlier framing of end-to-end mission explanation.

**[CSA Digital Games and Activities](https://www.asc-csa.gc.ca/eng/youth-educators/digital-games-and-activities.asp)**

Canadian Space Agency collection of digital games, e-books, videos, and hands-on activities for varied learners.

Suggested use: Explore how educational content introduces unfamiliar concepts.

Linked materials can have their own access conditions and licenses.
