## What the event is

NASA Space Apps Challenge is an annual, two-day global hackathon addressing problems on Earth and in space using NASA’s free, open data and Space Agency Partner resources. NASA subject matter experts author the challenges. People across ages, experience levels, and disciplines participate; programming is only one possible contribution. Projects can combine science, engineering, design, education, art, and storytelling.

The event also builds a community: subject matter experts contribute scientific guidance, Local Leads organize events, and Global Collaborators supply free tools and resources. The program is funded by NASA’s Science Mission Directorate through a contract with Booz Allen Hamilton, Mindgrub, and SecondMuse. [Official overview](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/about/)

The first event in 2012 involved approximately 2,000 people, 25 events, and 17 countries. Its scope later expanded to include more Earth science, arts, and humanities. [Official history](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/about/the-history-of-space-apps/)

## Participation, teams, and preparation

Participation is free. Under-18 participants need parent/legal-guardian registration and continuous accompaniment; Local Events may add in-person requirements. Space Apps Connect is an optional free forum for registered participants to find teammates and ask experts questions. [Participant FAQ](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/resources/participant-faqs/)

Create or sign into a Space Apps account, register for the 2026 event, and choose an in-person/virtual Local Event or the virtual Universal Event. The event page says registration remains open through the hackathon’s close; the terms require registration before participation begins, so register before starting your work. [2026 event page](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/2026/), [Participant terms](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/legal/)

Team rules:

- Local Event teams: 1–6 members. Universal Event teams: 2–6 for global judging.
- Each person may join one team; each team works on one official challenge.
- All members must be confirmed at the same event, rather than waitlisted, and listed on the team page.
- The Team Owner establishes the team’s event association and manages membership.
- The guide recommends varied skills and approximately four to five members.
- Review challenge statements and form teams beforehand, but do not begin project work before the official start.

Source: [Team Formation Guide](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/resources/team-formation-guide/).

## Official evaluation criteria

Every judging level uses the same five criteria, each scored **1–5**. The following condenses the rubric; the linked guide contains the full descriptions.

| Criterion | What judges assess |
| ------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Impact | Significance of the problem, effectiveness of the proposed solution, and benefit or inspiration to others |
| Creativity | Originality of the approach or a meaningful improvement on existing ideas |
| Validity | Scientific soundness; technical, functional, or logical feasibility; completeness; usability |
| Relevance | Direct response to the selected challenge, with NASA data contributing substantively |
| Presentation | Clear, organized, compelling communication of the problem, solution, and importance across the demo and full project |

The guide publishes no distinct percentage weights here. It encourages investment in presentation. Its highest validity descriptor emphasizes real-world readiness, although a fully developed product is not expected after two days.

Local/Universal judges select Global Nominees. Expert review selects Global Finalists and Honorable Mentions; Honorable Mentions do not advance. An executive committee evaluates finalists for ten Global Awards. A local award alone does not establish global advancement. [2026 Judging & Awards Guide](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/resources/judging-awards-guide/)

## Submission package

Judges primarily evaluate the Project page; the demo supplements it. Prepare:

- Project name, high-level summary, detailed description, final-project link, demo link, NASA-data explanation, tool-use disclosure, and references.
- At least one NASA dataset or resource for Global Award eligibility.
- A public demo: **up to seven slides**, including opening slides, **or a video no longer than 30 seconds**. Exceeding the limit prevents global judging.
- English submission text and linked materials; a demo video may use another spoken language with English subtitles.
- Public links accessible without login or permission. Host documents/videos externally; the page supports images up to 10 MB.
- Explicit submission with the required acknowledgements; saving progress alone is insufficient. Confirm the success banner. Editing/resubmission is allowed before closing.
- Test every link in a private browser window. The guide says most of the judges' evaluation rests on the Project page, and a strong demo raises the Presentation score.

**Suggested 30-second video timeline** (Project Submission Guide):

| Time | Content |
| --- | --- |
| 0:00 | Project name and team |
| 0:02 | The challenge and why you chose it |
| 0:05 | Project title and tagline |
| 0:10 | How it works: prototype visuals, the user experience, and how NASA or partner data made it possible |
| 0:20–0:30 | Future vision |

For a slide demo, the guide asks for slides that show the project, the user experience, and how NASA data is used, with large fonts and few words. A judges' post from 2017, [How to Develop a WINNING Space Apps Solution](https://2017.spaceappschallenge.org/blog/how-develop-winning-space-apps-solution), says the strongest teams treat presentation as seriously as the build, include storytelling and design skills, and present a next-steps or sustainability plan. It is older guidance; the 2026 guides take precedence.

The guide calls AI tools “Super Intelligence” (SI). It allows assistance but says SI should not originate the solution idea. Describe use; visibly watermark generated images/video and acknowledge generated audio/code/data in descriptive text and metadata. Generated content must not use or modify NASA branding. The disclosure field is not shown to judges; identified generated elements remain evaluable. [Project Submission Guide](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/resources/project-submission-guide/)

## Attribution, rights, and eligibility details

Original content must be freely available without restriction or use an OSI-compliant open-source license. If none is specified, NASA distributes original content under Apache 2.0. Third-party materials require compatible public availability/licensing, documented licenses, and citations for every resource—even freely available code, text, or images. Missing references can lead to removal or loss of awards.

Do not include confidential/proprietary information without publication rights, export-controlled information, or personal identifiers. Recognizable names, voices, and likenesses of under-18s must be excluded from submissions. NASA receives publication/use permissions under the terms, including publicity and media provisions.

Participation is voluntary and uncompensated. U.S. federal civil servants cannot register as participants. Restrictions also cover participation connected to China or Chinese-owned entities, including relevant institutional affiliations in Hong Kong/Macau; the terms distinguish affiliation from nationality. These are summaries: consult the complete terms for the applicable conditions. [2026 Participant Terms and Conditions](https://www.spaceappschallenge.org/legal/)

## Lessons from past winners

**Analysis, not official selection rules.** These patterns summarize NASA’s winner descriptions across 2023–2025. Winner-only descriptions cannot establish what caused success, actual technical maturity or learning gains, or a reliable probability of winning.

### Recurring patterns

| Recurring pattern | Examples | Planning implication |
| --------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Data becomes an action or decision | Waterwise, NVS-knot, SkySense, QUEÑARIS | Identify the decision the user can make after seeing the output. |
| Complex science becomes approachable | Spacebee, LunarTech Ensemble, Landsat Connect | Remove a specific obstacle to understanding or using scientific information. |
| Learning is interactive or narrative | TeamVoyagers, HerCode Space, Innovisionaries, Oogway Comics | Match the explanation format to the audience and learning goal. |
| A defined audience anchors the work | Researchers, farmers, children, visually impaired users, local communities | Choose a primary user and design one complete experience for them. |
| Both Earth and space problems receive recognition | Agricultural tools, climate exhibits, habitat design, exoplanet analysis | Select a problem the team can address convincingly with available evidence. |
| Different solutions to one challenge can win | 2023 moonquake projects; 2024 agricultural projects; three 2025 LEO projects | Distinguish the contribution, audience, or method within the challenge. |
| Advanced computation and creative media both appear | Seismic models, optimization, comics, sonification | Choose technology for its contribution to the result. |

An effective working proposition is: **“For [specific user], we use [specific NASA resource] to enable [decision or understanding], improving on [current approach] through [distinct contribution].”** This is a planning device, not a prescribed NASA submission format.

The examples do not establish that AI is necessary, that a fashionable topic is favored, or that one challenge has better odds.
